## From assistant to actor

The first wave of generative AI in banking was assistance: a chat window, a summary, a draft. Agentic AI changes the unit of work. Instead of answering a question, the system carries out a sequence: it pulls market and proprietary data, reasons over it, drafts the output and hands it to a banker for judgement. That is where the productivity gain compounds, and where the risk compounds too.

## Where it works

The strongest early uses share three features: several data sources, repeated structure, and a human who makes the final call. Examples include meeting preparation, investor and target screening, precedent research across transaction documents, and first drafts of client materials. The shared idea is the augmented banker: AI in every workflow, built on the firm's own data rather than on whichever model is in fashion.

## What has to be true first

- **Proprietary data, connected safely.** Agents are only as useful as the data they can reach. Orchestration layers such as MCP connect agents to internal and market data while controlling access and keeping data inside the firm.
- **Traceable steps.** Every action and source should be logged, so a reviewer can see how an output was produced.
- **Human sign-off at the right point.** Not on every step, which destroys the gain, but where a decision reaches a client or the market.
- **Measured reliability.** Multi-step systems fail in multi-step ways. Reliability has to be measured per workflow, not assumed from a model's benchmark.

The economics are set by review burden. As [The Checking Problem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28666) shows, an AI workflow's value depends less on how often it is right than on how much of it a human must still check.
